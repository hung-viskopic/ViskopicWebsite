Should cities ban private cars from their centres?

Many European cities now restrict cars in their historic centres, and the idea is spreading. Air pollution in dense areas is a measurable health problem, and public transport in most large cities is already good enough to replace many car journeys. In this essay I argue that a ban is justified, although it must be phased in with care.

The strongest reason for a ban is air quality. Road traffic is the main source of nitrogen dioxide in city centres. Monitoring in several European capitals has found levels above the legal limit beside busy roads. Therefore reducing traffic directly reduces the pollution that people breathe. Children and older residents suffer most, because their lungs are more sensitive.

A second reason is safety and the use of space. Streets without cars are safer for pedestrians and cyclists. For example, after one capital closed its central avenue to traffic, the number of injuries on it fell sharply. The space freed from parking can be used for trees, markets and play areas, which makes the centre pleasant enough to attract visitors.

However, shops are the most common objection. Retailers fear that customers who arrive by car will go to out-of-town malls instead. This worry is not unreasonable, because some shops sell heavy goods that people cannot easily carry on a bus. Yet studies of pedestrianised streets usually find that footfall rises, since people who walk visit more shops and stay longer. The loss from drivers is therefore often smaller than the gain from walkers.

Another objection concerns residents with disabilities, who may depend on a car. A ban that ignores them would be unfair. The answer is a permit system, which keeps access for blue-badge holders and deliveries while removing ordinary through traffic. Such exceptions weaken the pollution benefit only slightly, because they cover a small share of all journeys.

Finally, the council budget matters. Parking fees and fines are a large source of income for many councils, and a ban would remove it. This is a real cost. However, the money saved on treating pollution-related illness and on road repairs may offset part of it, and a congestion charge on the edge of the zone could replace some of the lost fees.

In conclusion, the benefits to health and safety outweigh the costs to shops and to the council budget, provided that exceptions exist for people who need cars and the change is introduced gradually.
